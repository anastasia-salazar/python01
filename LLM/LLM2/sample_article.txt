The Four-Day Work Week: Lessons from a Year of Experiments

When a mid-sized software company in Leeds announced last year that its 200 employees would move to a four-day week with no cut in pay, many industry observers expected productivity to fall. Twelve months later, the company reports the opposite: project delivery times dropped by 8 percent, and staff turnover fell from 18 percent to just 6 percent.

The company is not alone. Across Europe and North America, hundreds of organizations have trialed compressed schedules, most following the "100-80-100" model: 100 percent of the pay for 80 percent of the time, in exchange for 100 percent of the output. Results from the largest coordinated trials show that most participating firms chose to keep the policy after the trial ended.

Supporters argue that the gains come from focus rather than hours. With one less day, teams cut low-value meetings, automated repetitive tasks and set clearer priorities. Employees reported better sleep, less burnout and more time for family and exercise, which in turn reduced sick days.

The model is not without challenges. Customer-facing businesses such as retail, healthcare and hospitality struggle to reduce hours without hiring more staff, since someone must still be available five or seven days a week. Some managers also found that the shorter week squeezed the same workload into fewer days, raising stress for certain roles. Critics warn that early trial results may be flattered by the enthusiasm of companies that volunteered to take part.

Experts recommend that organizations considering the change start with a time-limited pilot, define clear productivity metrics before it begins, and involve employees in redesigning workflows. Rotating schedules can help customer-facing teams maintain coverage.

As labor markets remain tight and workers increasingly value flexibility, the four-day week is shifting from a fringe idea to a serious retention tool. Whether it becomes the norm may depend less on whether it works in principle and more on whether managers are willing to rethink how work gets done.
