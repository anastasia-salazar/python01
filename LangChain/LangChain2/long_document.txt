Retrieval-augmented generation, usually shortened to RAG, is a technique that lets a large language model answer questions using documents it was never trained on. Instead of relying only on what the model memorized during training, a RAG system first searches a collection of documents for passages related to the question, then places those passages into the prompt so the model can read them before answering. This makes answers more accurate, easier to verify, and simple to keep up to date, because updating the system only requires adding or replacing documents rather than retraining the model.

Before documents can be searched, they must be split into smaller pieces called chunks. A chunk that is too large wastes space in the prompt and blurs several topics into one embedding, which makes search results less precise. A chunk that is too small loses the surrounding context that gives a sentence its meaning, so a retrieved fragment such as "it was increased to 40 percent" becomes useless without the sentence that says what was increased. Good chunking therefore balances precision against context, and the right size depends on how dense and how structured the source documents are.

Chunk overlap solves a subtle problem at the boundaries between chunks. When a splitter cuts text at a fixed size, an important idea can be cut in half, with its first part at the end of one chunk and its second part at the start of the next. By repeating a small amount of text from the end of each chunk at the beginning of the following chunk, overlap ensures that ideas crossing a boundary still appear whole in at least one chunk. The cost is a little duplicated storage, but the benefit is that retrieval no longer misses answers simply because they happened to fall on a split point.
